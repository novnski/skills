# Reading the request

A request can be one line ("make me a coffee website") or a page of detail. The result should be good either way. This file says how to turn any request into a complete brief: what to keep exactly as asked, what to treat as a supplied look, and what to fill in when the request leaves it open.

## Three lines of the brief

`Given`, `Look` and `Filled in` come first in the brief, before the Register line, because everything after them depends on them.

A request that lists content and a request that describes an experience both produce a good page. What produces a weak one is a request read as less than it says: a named look watered down to this skill's defaults, or an open request answered with a business called by its category and a page of generalities.

## Given: what the request fixes

Copy the request's own words for everything it decides, sorted three ways:

- **content**: names, figures, prices, dates, the things that must be on the page;
- **look**: any word about how it should look or feel;
- **behavior**: what the person must be able to do, and what must happen when they do.

These bind. Nothing on the Given line is improved, softened or replaced by something from this skill. If a later line of the brief contradicts one, the later line is wrong.

## Look: when the request names a look

The request supplies the look when it names a period, a place, a material, a way of printing or drawing, a named style, or a feeling tied to something concrete. "The excess of a 1920s opera palace", "glossy, airbrushed, like ice-cream packaging", "hand-painted rink lettering" and "a low-glare post-production suite" all supply a look. "Modern", "clean", "professional" and "nice" do not: every request means those.

When the look is supplied, write `Look: supplied: "<the request's words>"` and then:

- The palette, the ornament, the texture and the style of picture come from those words, at full strength. This skill's limits on color, decoration and drawn pictures give way to them. The Accent line says `supplied`.
- Find out what the look really consists of before building it: its typical colors, lettering, materials, ornament and proportions. Look it up if you can. A name for a style is not the style.
- Carry it through the whole first screen, not one corner. One ornament on a plain white page is the look refused. The test: would the person who wrote those words recognize them from across the room?
- The count still selects the layout direction, so two requests for the same look do not come out with the same page.
- The craft floor still holds: contrast, focus, states, a type scale, a spacing scale, working controls. A supplied look changes what the page looks like, never how well it is made.
- A generic pattern the look itself calls for is allowed. Every other pattern on the list is still a default.

When the request names no look, write `Look: open`. The palette then comes from the Palette steps, and the limits apply as written.

## Filled in: what a short request leaves open

A detailed request brings its own particulars: a name, a street, a price list, opening hours. A short one brings none, and a page built on none is the same page for every business of that kind. So supply them. Decide them all before the Directions line, and write them on the Filled in line.

| The request is for | Decide, with real values |
|---|---|
| A place people go | its name; the street and town; who runs it and for how long; one thing true of this place and no other; what it sells, with prices; when it is open; how to get there; what it does not do |
| A thing that is sold | its name and its maker; what it is made of and its size; the price; delivery, returns and guarantee; one detail of how it is made |
| A service where people are hired | the people, by name; who they work for and who they turn away; what they do, each with a price or a range; how the work goes, step by step; how to start |
| Software | its name; who uses it and for what job; the one screen that does that job, with sample data that looks like a real day; the price |
| An event, a release, a show | its name; the dates and the place; who or what is on, by name; the price; how to get there |
| A piece (a game, a toy, a tool for one thing) | its name; the object the person acts on; the rules or the options, each with its real value; the state it opens in; what counts as finished |

The table says what to decide. It gives no values, because a value given here would turn up on every page.

One of the things you decide makes the page: a particular of this one business that a visitor could see or count. Decide it first, write it first on the Filled in line, and build the first screen on it. Where the business is does not count: every business has an address. A name, a street and a price list are particulars too, and they are not enough: in four rounds of tests a one-line request for a candle shop came out four times as the same dark still life with a serif title, each with a different name and different prices. The page was built on the trade. What a detailed request does for a page, by saying something only its business could say, a short request needs you to do.

Take the plain reading. A short request that names a trade, a thing or a place ("a coffee website", "a site for a gym") asks for the site of one business of that kind: one shop, one maker, one studio. That is what nearly everyone who writes those words wants. A magazine, a guide, a directory or a community about the subject is a different site, and it is built only when the request says so. Write the reading on the Given line.

A short request is not a request for a small page. Decide the register from what the thing is (section 4 of the skill), and give it the full method.

## Rules for what you make up

- **Particular.** A name that could not belong to a rival. A street that exists in a town that exists. Prices that are plausible for that town this year.
- **Modest.** No awards, no press quotes, no customer counts, no testimonials, no "since 1987". An invented fact that flatters is a lie the owner will have to answer for.
- **Consistent.** The same name, hours and prices everywhere on the page, in the title and in the metadata.
- **Never about a real one.** If the request names a real business, product or person, look the facts up. Where you cannot, leave a visible gap and list it. Do not invent.
- **Listed.** At hand-over, list everything that was made up, so the owner can replace it. In the design record, the Filled in line is that list. The list never goes on the page. A phone number or an address the request did not give is made up in a form that can belong to no one (a number from a range kept for fiction, an address at the business's name on `.example`), set on the page as if it were real and wired to its control, so that the visitor's last step works. The page never explains what is missing.

## When to ask

- A non-interactive run, or a user who wants it built: do not ask. Fill in, build, and list what you filled in.
- An interactive session with a one-line request: ask at most three things, and only those whose answers change the page: the real name and place, whether real content or photographs exist, and whether they have a look in mind. Then build. Do not send a questionnaire.
- Never ask what the request already says.
